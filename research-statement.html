Research statement

Looking inward through the machine: need discovery in human-AI communication

Information science has known for a long time that people can't fully say what they need before they start looking. Taylor (1968) called the reference interview "question negotiation"; Belkin described the searcher's anomalous state of knowledge. Generative AI is the newest version of this old problem — except now the system doesn't retrieve answers, it generates responses that change the question itself. And because frontier models move faster than any curriculum can keep up with, there is no settled way to "learn AI." People learn by doing.

My thesis is that in human-AI communication, people don't just express needs they already have. They figure out what their needs are through the interaction. Each round of back-and-forth sharpens the need. The machine works like a mirror — for looking inward.

I come to this question as a practitioner. Since May 2026 I have published 43 bilingual (English/Chinese) AI tutorial videos on Douyin, about 3.2 million views in total, including a 12-video series on AI agents — alongside brand partnerships promoting agent products. My comment sections are a live record of need discovery: thousands of users showing, in their own words, the moment a vague impulse turns into a real question. Or doesn't. Watch time, comments, shares — the backend data lets me trace that process at a scale no lab study could match.

As Co-Founder & Marketing Lead of AI Markethon, a competition that helps AI products reach real users, I see the industry side of the same problem: good products dying at the last mile. Not because the technology fails — because users can't figure out what to ask of it.

I am turning this material into a working paper: my own creator practice as the deep case — public metrics, backend engagement data, comment threads — set against a comparison sample of other AI creators. The question is how creators model the inward-discovery process for their audiences, and which approaches actually help audiences learn rather than just copy prompts.

The doctoral project I want to build has three questions at its core, all rooted in information behavior.

First, discovery in interaction: how do people discover and clarify what they need through repeated exchanges with AI? What does the path from vague impulse to a precise, usable need actually look like?

Second, scaffolding the inward turn: when creators teach, what are they really teaching — tool skills, or the harder work of self-clarification? Which kinds of guidance help audiences look inward, and which just hand them prompts to paste?

Third, from discovery to adoption: how does figuring out what you want turn into sustained, everyday use? And when the machine helps define the need, who is really directing whom?

I focus on short-video audiences, especially Chinese-speaking users, who meet AI almost entirely through creator explanations — a huge population that barely shows up in adoption research or information-behavior work. My TESOL training turns out to be directly relevant: language teaching is the science of helping people express meaning, and what I study — discovering what you think by trying to say it — is something language teachers have watched happen for decades. For non-native speakers the problem doubles: a language gap and a technology gap at once.

At bottom these are questions about how people seek information, make sense of it, and act on it — now playing out between humans and AI. The I School's tradition of studying people, information, and technology together is where this work belongs. I am drawn to Professor Kapoor's work on AI as Normal Technology; my question is what that diffusion looks like at the level of everyday communication. And to Professor Salehi's research on human agency in AI workflows — which gets at exactly what is at stake when a machine helps define your need.

What I bring is an unusual field site and a practitioner's dataset. What I need from doctoral training is rigor: computational content analysis, interviews, surveys, research design — the tools to turn field access into knowledge that generalizes. I think of myself as methods-in-training, with a real empirical project already underway. I also plan to keep writing for the public. The audiences I study are people I can actually reach.
