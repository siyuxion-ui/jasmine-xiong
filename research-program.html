Research program

What I've been circling, and what I'd like to study

Over the past year I made 43 short videos teaching ordinary people how to use AI, in Chinese and English, mostly on Douyin — about 3.2 million views in total. I didn't start out doing research. But after a few thousand comments, I kept noticing something that has very little to do with how capable the models are.

People don't arrive with a clear task. They arrive with a vague itch: everyone says AI can help with work, but what am I supposed to ask it? The hard part is never the tool. It's figuring out what you want in the first place.

That observation is the thread connecting everything I want to study. Put bluntly: the gap between what AI can do and what people actually do with it isn't a technology problem. It's a communication problem. Information science has been circling this question for decades — Robert Taylor called it "question negotiation," Nicholas Belkin called it the anomalous state of knowledge. People can't fully say what they need before they start looking. Generative AI just makes the old problem sharper, because the system's answers change the question itself.

Three directions, from the largest scale down to the smallest.

1. How people figure out what they need, by talking with AI.

Frontier models move too fast for any stable curriculum. There is no settled body of "AI knowledge" to teach, so people learn in practice — and what they're really learning isn't prompt technique. It's how to recognize and say what they want. I watch this happen in my comment sections every day: someone shows up with a fuzzy need, and through rounds of trying, failing, and rephrasing with the machine, they land on what they were actually after. The machine works like a mirror.

My TESOL background turns out to be more relevant than it looks. Language teaching is, at bottom, the work of helping people express meaning. Helping a student find words for a half-formed thought and helping a user find a prompt for a half-formed need are the same kind of work.

2. Who does the teaching when no one can keep up.

There's no manual for AI, and by the time a course gets designed, the models have already changed. So ordinary people learn from creators. People like me have quietly become the customer support, translators, and teachers of AI adoption. I want to study that informal infrastructure properly: how it earns trust, and what money does to it. I've taken brand deals myself. I know how a sponsorship can quietly bend an explanation. When does teaching become advertising, and can the audience tell the difference? As a creator and as co-founder and marketing lead of an AI product launch competition, I can see both sides of that question.

3. Why AI spreads further in some places than others.

A puzzle I've been turning over: the country building the strongest models isn't the one where AI has spread furthest into everyday life. American AI discourse runs elite — parameters, benchmarks, capability talk. Chinese AI discourse, at least on Douyin, runs vernacular — ordinary people showing each other how to use Doubao for daily errands. Capability doesn't equal diffusion. The route the message travels matters. My hunch is that the vernacular route lowers the bar for the inward figuring-out I described above — a concrete life scenario shows you what to want — while the elite route quietly assumes you already know. I make content for both worlds, so this is a comparison I can actually run.

Why this matters beyond my own curiosity.

Two reasons. The theoretical one: most HCI and AI evaluation research starts with a user who already knows their task. I think the more important bottleneck sits before the task is ever defined, and the benchmarks have the same blind spot. The equity one: if adopting AI takes elite technical literacy, AI widens the gap; if it can ride on vernacular communication, it democratizes. There's also a fork worth naming — AI as a mirror that helps you think things through, versus AI as a nanny that decides for you.

Where I'd value your guidance.

If you had to pick one: which of these three feels most promising, and most worth building into a dissertation-scale project? And is there a fourth angle I'm not seeing?
